## 1. Scope and method

This covers the four Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct (TDRPC) that govern conflicts of interest for a law firm's intake and matter-opening process: Rule 1.06 (current clients), Rule 1.09 (former clients), Rule 1.10 (imputation to the firm), and Rule 1.18 (prospective clients — i.e., exactly the "person who called and never became a client" case this product's intake flow creates constantly). Texas-only, consistent with `c1`, `c2`, and `c75`'s scoping and with `c28`'s not-yet-narrowed launch-state decision.

Sourced from the Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct as currently in force, cross-checked against reporting on the Supreme Court of Texas's 2024 rules referendum for amendment and effective-date history. Full citations in §8. Where a secondary source paraphrases rather than quotes the rule verbatim, that is noted — an attorney reviewer should confirm exact rule language against the official Texas Bar / Supreme Court of Texas publication before any wording here is treated as authoritative.

## 2. A rule-numbering and currency flag, same pattern as `c10` and `c75`

Every other compliance document on this board has had to flag a rule-numbering or recency issue (`c10`: Rule 5.04/5.05 renumbering; `c75`: Rule 1.15 vs. the older "1.14" citation some secondary sources still use). This document has its own version, and it is a bigger deal than a renumbering — it's new law:

- **Rule 1.09** (former-client conflicts) was **amended**, effective **October 1, 2024**, per the Supreme Court of Texas's order on the 2024 rules referendum.
- **Rule 1.10** (imputation of conflicts of interest to the whole firm) is a **new rule**, also effective October 1, 2024. Texas did not previously have a standalone imputation rule numbered 1.10 in the way the ABA Model Rules do; imputation was previously folded into Rule 1.06(f) and Rule 1.09(b).
- **Rule 1.18** (duties to a prospective client) is likewise a **new rule**, effective October 1, 2024. Texas previously had no dedicated prospective-client rule at all — this is the single most relevant fact in this entire document for an intake product, because it means the legal framework for "someone called, we never engaged them, do we owe them anything" is **barely a year old** in Texas as of this writing (today is 2026-10-14).
- **Rule 1.06** (current-client conflicts, the "lawyer shall not represent opposing parties to the same litigation" rule) does not appear in reporting on the 2024 referendum and is treated here as unchanged by it, but an attorney reviewer should confirm this directly against the official rule text rather than relying on the absence of a mention in secondary reporting.

**Why this matters for the engine specifically:** `c3`'s role matrix and tri-state logic (clear / possible / definite) were very likely designed using general knowledge of conflicts-of-interest practice rather than this specific, newly-adopted Texas rule text — Rule 1.10's and Rule 1.18's exact screening and notice mechanics (§4, §5 below) did not exist as named Texas rules before October 2024. This is exactly the kind of gap `c26`'s eventual attorney reviewer should be asked to check line-by-line against the shipped engine, not assumed to already be handled correctly.

## 3. Rule 1.06 — Conflict of Interest: General Rule (current clients)

Paraphrased from secondary sources (RuleDex); an attorney reviewer should confirm against the official text:

- **(a)** A lawyer may not represent opposing parties to the same litigation. This is treated as close to absolute.
- **(b)** Outside that litigation bar, a lawyer generally may not take on a representation if it either (1) involves a matter substantially related to one in which the new client's interests are materially and directly adverse to an existing client of the lawyer or firm, or (2) reasonably appears to be, or could become, adversely limited by the lawyer's or firm's responsibilities to another client, a third person, or the lawyer's own interests.
- **(c)** A (b)-type conflict can be waived if the lawyer reasonably believes the representation of each affected client won't be materially affected, and each client consents after full disclosure.
- **(d)** A lawyer who represented multiple parties in a matter generally cannot later represent any of them against each other in a dispute arising from that same matter, absent consent from all of them.
- **(e)** A lawyer must promptly withdraw from a representation that violates this rule or becomes improper under it.
- **(f)** A conflict that disqualifies one lawyer generally disqualifies every other lawyer in that lawyer's firm (the general imputation principle Rule 1.10 now elaborates on specifically).

**Engine implication:** this is the rule behind the engine's "role-sensitive" requirement already noted on the board (`c3`'s card text: "the same prior contact that is harmless for representation can bar the firm from mediating between those parties, so matches are evaluated against the role being sought, not just matched by name") — 1.06(b)(1)'s "materially and directly adverse" test and (d)'s "represented multiple parties, now a dispute between them" scenario are both role-dependent, not name-matching. §6 below gives this as a concrete worked rule for the engine.

## 4. Rule 1.09 — Conflict of Interest: Former Client (amended 2024-10-01)

Paraphrased from secondary sources; an attorney reviewer should confirm the amended text specifically, since the pre-2024 version is what most non-current secondary sources describe:

- **(a)** A lawyer who formerly represented a client in a matter generally cannot later represent another person in the same or a substantially related matter where that person's interests are materially adverse to the former client, unless the former client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.
- **(b)** A lawyer cannot knowingly take on a matter adverse to a former client of a *prior firm* the lawyer used to belong to, if the lawyer personally acquired protected confidential information (under Rule 1.05, and 1.09(c)) that is material to the new matter. Same consent exception.
- **(c)** Confidentiality survives the end of the representation: a lawyer (or present/former firm) cannot use information from a former representation to that former client's disadvantage, or reveal it, except as the rules otherwise permit or the information is generally known.

**"Substantially related"** (per the comments, paraphrased) turns on whether the new matter involves the same transaction or dispute, or whether there is a substantial risk that confidential factual information the lawyer normally would have learned in the first representation would materially advance the new client's position in the second.

**Engine implication:** this is the rule behind the board's own framing — "a prior consult is disqualifying even where no engagement followed" overstates Rule 1.09 slightly (1.09 is about *former clients*, i.e., representations that actually happened); the prospective-client-only case (a consult that never became a representation) is governed by Rule 1.18 instead (§5). The engine's screening logic should distinguish these two categories (former client under 1.09 vs. prospective-only contact under 1.18) rather than treating "any prior contact" as one undifferentiated bucket, because the rules impose meaningfully different tests (1.09's "substantially related matter" vs. 1.18's "information that could be significantly harmful").

## 5. Rule 1.10 — Imputation of Conflicts of Interest: General Rule (new, 2024-10-01)

Paraphrased from secondary sources:

- **(a)** Lawyers in a firm generally cannot knowingly represent a client if any one of them, practicing alone, would be barred from doing so under Rule 1.06 or Rule 1.09 — with two exceptions:
  1. The bar arises from a lawyer's *personal* interest and doesn't present a significant risk of materially limiting the other firm lawyers' representation; or
  2. The bar arises under Rule 1.09(a) or (b) from a lawyer's **prior firm**, and the now-conflicted lawyer is **timely screened**, receives **no part of the fee** from the matter, and the former client gets **prompt written notice** describing the screen and the firm's compliance with it.
- **(b)** A firm may take on a matter materially adverse to a lawyer who has since left the firm, unless the new matter is the same or substantially related to the departed lawyer's prior work *and* a remaining lawyer at the firm holds material protected information about it.
- **(c)** The affected client may waive a Rule 1.06-type disqualification.
- **(d)** Government lawyers are instead governed by Rule 1.11.

**Engine implication — this is the rule `c60`'s "ethical screens (walls)" card exists to enforce.** The screening mechanism in (a)(2) has concrete, checkable requirements a system can actually verify rather than merely document: (1) the screen must be timely (in place before the conflicted lawyer has any further access to the new matter — the engine should be able to show *when* the screen was imposed relative to matter intake), (2) the screened lawyer must get **no part of the fee** specifically tied to that matter (a billing-system check, not just an HR policy — ties directly to `c77`/`c79`'s eventual billing and invoicing work: a screened lawyer's time or fee share on a screened matter should be a hard, system-enforced block, not a manual reminder), and (3) **prompt written notice** to the former client describing the screen (a generated, trackable document/notification, not a verbal assurance). All three are things `c60`'s design should treat as structured, auditable facts (a `screen_imposed_at` timestamp, a billing-exclusion flag tied to the matter and lawyer, and a notice-sent record in the audit trail per `c6`) rather than a free-text policy description.

## 6. Rule 1.18 — Duties to Prospective Client (new, 2024-10-01)

Paraphrased from secondary sources — the single most directly relevant rule to this product's own intake funnel:

- **(a) Who counts as a "prospective client":** someone who, **in good faith**, consults a lawyer about possibly forming a client-lawyer relationship. The comments reportedly add that a unilateral submission of information — e.g., someone replying to general advertising without a good-faith intent to actually retain the firm — does **not** by itself create prospective-client status. Someone who contacts a lawyer *only* to try to disqualify that lawyer or firm from an adverse representation is explicitly carved out too.
- **(b) Confidentiality:** even if no client relationship ever forms, information learned from a prospective client cannot be used or revealed, subject to the same exceptions that apply to actual clients (informed consent, generally known information, and so on) — and per the comments, this duty attaches "regardless of how brief the initial conference may be."
- **(c) Conflicts:** a lawyer generally cannot thereafter act against that prospective client in the same or a substantially related matter if the lawyer received information from them that could be **significantly harmful** to them in that matter. Other lawyers at the same firm are generally barred too (imputation, tying back to Rule 1.10).
- **(d) Exceptions:** representation may proceed with informed consent, confirmed in writing, from both the affected current/new client and the prospective client — or, alternatively, if the lawyer took reasonable steps to limit their own exposure to the harmful information, the exposed lawyer is **timely screened** and takes **no part of the fee**, and the prospective client gets **prompt written notice**.

**Engine implications — several, all directly load-bearing for `c3`'s "POSSIBLE" tri-state outcome:**

1. **The "significantly harmful information" test, not a flat name-match.** Rule 1.18(c) does not disqualify the firm merely because someone once called; it disqualifies on *information exposure that could be significantly harmful in that specific matter*. A system that treats every prior inbound contact as automatically disqualifying is **over-blocking** relative to the actual rule — which may be the right conservative default for an AI-driven system that cannot itself judge "how harmful," but it should be a deliberate, attorney-reviewed design choice (bias toward the POSSIBLE/escalate state rather than auto-clearing) rather than an accident of the rule being misread as "any contact disqualifies."
2. **Good-faith intake vs. advertising reply.** (a)'s carve-out for people who weren't acting in good faith, or who only replied to general advertising, is a judgment call this product should **never** have the AI make unilaterally — it is exactly the kind of fact-specific legal determination `c1`'s UPL review already drew a line around. The engine's safest design is to treat *every* inbound contact that reached the conversational intake flow (`c12`) as a prospective client for conflict-check purposes, and let a human (not the AI) decide later, case by case, whether a specific contact actually falls outside Rule 1.18(a)'s scope. Recommendation: do not build an automated "good faith" filter into the conflict-check engine.
3. **The screening mechanism is identical in shape to Rule 1.10's** (timely screen, no fee share, prompt written notice) — the same structured, auditable facts recommended in §5 apply here too, and a single shared implementation inside `c60` should serve both Rule 1.10(a)(2) and Rule 1.18(d)'s screening paths rather than building two parallel ones.
4. **"Regardless of how brief"** — the confidentiality duty attaches from the first message of a chat session, not from some later "qualifying" point in the conversation. The conversational intake flow (`c12`) and its disclosure/consent card (`c72`) should both treat the conversation as confidentiality-protected from message one, which is also consistent with `c72`'s own note about disclosures needing to run "before any questions, on every channel."

## 7. A concrete rule for the engine to implement (recommended, not final)

Framed the same way `c75` §11 translated its research into an implementation checklist — this is a recommendation for the attorney reviewer to confirm or correct, not a settled specification:

1. Every inbound contact that reaches the conversational intake flow is logged as either (a) an actual client (an engagement happened), (b) a former client (a past engagement ended), or (c) a prospective client under Rule 1.18 (contact happened, no engagement) — never silently dropped from the conflict index once contact occurred, matching `c3`'s existing note that "closed matters stay in the conflict index permanently."
2. A conflict check screens the caller **and** the opposing party (already noted on `c3`'s card) against all three categories above, not just "current/former clients" — a prospective-client-only contact is still a real conflict risk under Rule 1.18(c), even though no engagement ever existed.
3. The check is role-sensitive: the same prior contact is evaluated differently depending on whether the firm would now represent that person, represent someone adverse to them, or attempt to mediate between them and someone else (Rule 1.06(d)'s multiple-party-dispute scenario is a third, distinct role case beyond "for" or "against").
4. A match against **former-client** records (Rule 1.09) or **prospective-client** records (Rule 1.18) where the new matter is the same or substantially related should default to the engine's **POSSIBLE** state — escalate to attorney review — rather than an automatic DEFINITE block, because both rules turn on a fact-specific "materially adverse" / "significantly harmful information" test the AI cannot responsibly resolve alone (this matches `c3`'s existing design intent per its card text: "the engine never resolves this itself").
5. A screen imposed under Rule 1.10(a)(2) or Rule 1.18(d) is tracked as three auditable facts per screened lawyer per matter: when the screen was imposed, a hard billing-exclusion flag (ties to `c77`'s time entries and `c79`'s invoices — a screened lawyer's entries on that matter should be blocked at write time, not caught later), and a record that written notice was sent to the affected former/prospective client (audit trail, `c6`).
6. None of the above authorizes the AI to make the underlying legal judgment (is this "substantially related," is this information "significantly harmful") — those stay attorney calls, surfaced through the POSSIBLE state, consistent with `c1`'s UPL guardrails.
